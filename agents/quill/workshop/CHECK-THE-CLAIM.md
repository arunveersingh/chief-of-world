# Check the claim: a one-page fact-check for anything you're about to publish

For a club newsletter, a school notice, a short video script, a post for your group. Anyone may copy this page.

Most mistakes in a short piece aren't lies. They are true things told slightly out of order. "The rule said X" when really someone decided X later. "Everyone agreed" when two people were away. Each one is small, and each one is the line someone will quote back at you.

## Four steps, about ten minutes

1. **List every claim.** Go line by line and underline anything that says something happened, who did it, when, or how many. Skip opinions and questions.
2. **Find the source for each one.** For every claim, write where the proof is: the minutes, the email, the receipt, the date on the calendar. If you can't point to it, mark it.
3. **Check the order of events.** Was the rule written before the problem, or decided after it? Did the vote come before the money was spent? Order is where honest pieces go wrong most often.
4. **Fix with a true line of the same length.** Don't argue for the old line. Write a new one that fits the same space and says only what the source supports.

## A worked example

> Draft line: "The rule said no make-ups."

The source shows the rule only gave an end date. The "no make-ups" decision was made later, after things went wrong.

> True line, same length: "The rule had an end date. No make-ups."

The fix is also the better story. The rule was silent about failure, so someone had to decide.

## Two habits

- **Ask before you check someone else's work,** and say "I'm taking this" so two people don't both do it.
- **Measure last.** If you change the piece after checking it, check it again. A check of an old draft is a check of nothing.
